# Classroom guide

**The Butterfly Effect: The Cost of Every Choice** is a complete narrative economics game for high school seniors. Its four chapters follow a fictional graduate from age 18 to 22. It has no winner/loser score.

## Start a session

Open the published game link in Chrome. Students choose a starting interest and begin, or continue the save on that device. No account or personal information is needed. Saves do not travel between browsers or devices. On shared devices, start a fresh story between students.

Students may walk with WASD/arrows and interact with E/Enter, or use the Locations menu for every essential interaction. Listen reads the current panel and all choices. Slower narration and reduced motion are available.

The reading and decisions are designed for a 25–35-minute session, with optional exploration. Actual duration depends on reading, narration speed, and how many optional conversations students choose; this is a design target, not a measured class result. Students do not need to complete every location each day.

## The four chapters

1. **After graduation (18):** fourteen short days introduce family, work, training, housing, food, transport, and the Albuquerque neighborhood. Students can finish a day whenever ready.
2. **Finding a rhythm (19–20):** work/learning direction, a schedule conflict, and housing. The starred location contains the main decision.
3. **Changing plans (20–21):** transportation, a clinic appointment, and a new work opportunity. Prior support and preparation can change access.
4. **Looking ahead (21–22):** rent increases, learning or changing direction, and a final priority. At 22, students select an important decision and write a short class reflection.

After the opening, **Continue the story** jumps between important moments. Each has one required decision, optional neighborhood visits, and a visible representative monthly budget. Students preview the budget before settling it. The game does not secretly simulate every month between ages 18 and 22.

## Economics to notice

- Scarcity includes seats, tools, appointment slots, privacy, services, and support—not only cash or the clock.
- Opportunity cost is the next-best alternative given up, not every possible alternative combined.
- A financial shortfall can reflect wages, access, rent, care needs, or an unexpected constraint. Avoid asking students to rank pathways by virtue.
- Work, education, rest, connection, and independence can each matter. An extra dollar is not the only meaningful consequence.
- The monetary amounts and lending rules are illustrative. Loan interest is 6% APR applied only to the selected modeled months. Unpaid bills and loan principal are shown separately in the final snapshot.

## Ending and reflection

The age-22 snapshot summarizes direction, learning, home, travel, finances, earlier decisions, and relationships. Students choose a decision for their significant opportunity cost and answer:

1. What resource was scarce?
2. What was your next-best alternative?
3. What support or barrier changed your options?

**Download Life Snapshot** saves a text file locally; **Print snapshot** opens the browser print dialog. Students should avoid names or personal information in their answers. Answers save on the device as they type. The full journal remains available from the snapshot.

For a discussion, compare two students who chose differently: What did each value? What resources were available to each? What public service or structural change could have expanded both sets of options? Do not compare final balances as a success ranking.

## Continuing an existing game

A completed earlier two-week version can now continue to chapter 2. No restart is required. An existing first-week or graduation-day save also continues. A final save returns directly to the age-22 snapshot and saved reflection. Students may download it before starting another pathway.
